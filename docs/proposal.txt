Class Balancing and Calibration for Toxicity Prediction on New
Chemical Scaffolds
Muhammad Zohaib Chaudhary
CSC606 Machine Learning | Individual Project Proposal

1 Problem and objectives
I plan to test whether balancing the training data helps machine learning models find active
compounds with unfamiliar chemical structures. I will also check how reliable their predicted
probabilities are. The project will use three Tox21 laboratory assays and compare random forests
with logistic regression in Google Colab.
Active compounds are much less common than inactive compounds in these assays. A model can
get many predictions right simply by predicting that most compounds are inactive. Balancing may
help it find more active cases, but it can also affect its probability estimates. For example, if a
model assigns 100 compounds a probability close to 0.20, about 20 should be active for that
estimate to be reliable.
My research question is: Which balancing methods help models find more active compounds on
new chemical scaffolds while keeping their probability estimates reliable? A scaffold is the central
framework of rings and connecting groups in a molecule. I will compare methods using the same
number of compounds selected for screening, then check their probabilities before and after
calibration. The aim is to help choose compounds for laboratory testing. An active assay result
alone does not establish whether a chemical is harmful to people.
2 Preliminary literature review
Idakwo et al. [1], including coauthor Chaoyang Zhang, studied imbalanced Tox21 assays using
random forests, random undersampling, SMOTE, and SMOTEENN. They reported Brier scores as
well as classification measures. SMOTEENN performed well overall, which makes it a useful
method to include in my comparison.
Santiago-Gonzalez et al. [2] compared balancing methods across ten Tox21 assays, several ways
of representing molecules, and five classifiers. Their study already covers a broad range of
methods. I will focus on whether balancing still helps when the test compounds have scaffolds that
are absent from the training data.
Van den Goorbergh et al. [3] found that resampling could distort logistic-regression probabilities
without improving the separation of positive and negative cases. Their evidence came from
simulations and a clinical case study. This gives a reason to check calibration, but the same result
cannot be assumed for chemical fingerprints or random forests.
The September 2026 ToxBench study [4] examines random and scaffold splits, probability
calibration, and structural similarity. It also uses class weighting for its tree models. I will build on
that evaluation setup by comparing balancing choices, including a model trained without
balancing, and applying the same calibration procedure to each. The question is whether balancing
improves detection on unfamiliar structures and what happens to the probabilities it produces.

3 Data and preparation
I will use the public Tox21 CSV file from DeepChem [5]. A preliminary check of the download
found 7,831 records, SMILES strings that describe molecular structures, and twelve assay-label
columns. I selected NR-AR, NR-AhR, and SR-MMP because they have different proportions of
active compounds and cover nuclear-receptor and stress-response assays. Table 1 shows the raw
counts. These will change after cleaning the structures and removing duplicates.

    NR-AR         7,265                  309          4.25%               566
    NR-AhR        6,549                  768          11.73%              1,282
   SR-MMP         5,810                  918          15.80%              2,021

NR-AR measures androgen-receptor activity, NR-AhR measures aryl-hydrocarbon-receptor
activity, and SR-MMP concerns mitochondrial membrane potential. Each assay will be a separate
binary prediction task. If a label is missing, that compound will be excluded only from the affected
assay. Missing labels will not be treated as inactive results.
RDKit [6] will parse the SMILES strings, standardize structures, retain the main organic fragment,
and generate canonical identifiers. Invalid structures will be logged and excluded. For each assay,
duplicate structures with consistent labels will be merged, while structures with conflicting labels
will be removed from that assay. These rules will be fixed before splitting. I will save the
download checksum and a record of all exclusions.
4 Proposed machine learning approach
Each molecule will be represented by a 2,048-bit Morgan fingerprint with radius 2, commonly
called ECFP4. This encodes patterns in its local chemical structure. I will use the same
representation throughout so that changes in the results can be traced to the balancing method.
Random forests can learn nonlinear combinations of these patterns. Regularized logistic regression
will provide a simpler comparison.
For each classifier, I will compare no balancing, inverse-frequency class weights, random
undersampling, and SMOTEENN [7]. Undersampling will reduce the majority class to reach a 1:1
ratio. SMOTEENN will first oversample toward 1:1, then remove points using its nearest-
neighbor cleaning rule. I will report the final counts after cleaning. The synthetic fingerprint
vectors are numerical training examples, not newly generated molecules.
I will test four settings for each classifier. For logistic regression, C will be 0.01, 0.1, 1, or 10.
Random forests will use 200 trees, with maximum depth 10 or unrestricted and minimum leaf size
1 or 5. Resampling and class weights will be calculated using training folds only. Evaluation folds
will retain their observed class proportions.

5 Evaluation plan
The main evaluation will hold out chemical scaffolds. Compounds sharing a Bemis-Murcko
scaffold will remain together, including one group for acyclic compounds with an empty scaffold.
Groups will be assigned to approximately 60% training, 20% calibration, and 20% testing. Exact
sizes and positive counts will be reported. A stratified random split with the same proportions will
provide a reference. Both settings will use three fixed seeds and identical partitions for all
competing methods.
Hyperparameters will be selected by average precision in three-fold cross-validation within the
training partition. The scaffold experiment will preserve scaffold groups in these folds; the random
experiment will use stratified folds. Any learned preprocessing and resampling will be fitted inside
each fold. The selected model will then be refitted on the training partition. Neither the calibration
partition nor the test partition will be used for model selection.
I will fit a sigmoid calibration function [8] using the separate calibration partition, which will
retain its original class frequencies. This function adjusts model scores to improve their agreement
with observed activity rates. Every method, including the model without balancing, will be tested
before and after calibration. Test labels will be used only for evaluation. If calibration preserves
the ranking of compounds, I will not count it as an improvement in ranking.
The main detection measure will be average precision, which summarizes the precision-recall
curve. I will also measure precision and recall among the highest-ranked 10% of test compounds,
with a fixed rule for ties. Selecting the same proportion gives each method the same screening
budget. ROC-AUC will be a secondary measure. Each results table will include the proportion of
active compounds because it affects precision-based measures.
I will assess the probabilities using Brier score and log loss. A model that always predicts the
training prevalence will provide a simple reference. Reliability plots, with the number of
compounds in each bin, will show how predicted probabilities compare with observed activity
rates. Since Brier score measures more than calibration, I will read it alongside these plots. I will
report results before and after calibration, including any cases where it makes them worse.
Each balancing method will be compared with the model without balancing on the same assay,
data split, and calibration condition. I will report means and standard deviations across seeds, plus
paired 95% bootstrap intervals within each test partition. Test scaffold groups will be resampled
together. Results for subsets with fewer than 20 active compounds will be treated as exploratory. I
will also measure Tanimoto similarity to the nearest training compound, since molecules with
different scaffolds can still be similar.
I will look for methods that find more active compounds within the screening budget while
retaining reliable probabilities after calibration. If a method improves detection but makes its
probabilities worse, I will report both results. Comparing the random and scaffold splits will show
whether a benefit remains when the model is tested on unfamiliar scaffolds.

6 Google Colab implementation and schedule
The Colab notebook will use Python, Pandas, NumPy, RDKit, scikit-learn, imbalanced-learn, and
Matplotlib. Pandas can load the compressed CSV directly from DeepChem. I will use the official
data loader and library examples [5-8] as references, then write the cleaning, data-splitting,
training, and evaluation code for this project.
The plan includes 144 fitted model configurations: three assays, two classifiers, four balancing
choices, two split methods, and three seeds. The cross-validation searches require additional fits,
while calibration reuses the fitted models. I will first measure the time needed for one assay on a
Colab CPU. Fingerprints and resampled training folds will be cached, and completed predictions
will be saved between sessions. The dataset size and small tuning grids should keep the work
manageable without a GPU.

7 Expected outcomes and research value
The study should help explain when balancing finds more active compounds and when it mainly
changes the predicted probabilities. I expect performance on unfamiliar scaffolds to be weaker or
less stable than on a random split. The experiments will show whether calibration helps, and
whether any improvement depends on the assay or classifier.
I will produce a Colab notebook that others can rerun, with fixed data splits and the same
evaluation rules for every method. The final report will compare results for each assay and include
precision-recall and reliability plots. The notebook will contain the full tables, graphs, and code. I
will discuss small active-class counts, noisy assay labels, and the limits of measuring chemical
similarity with fingerprints.
For a PhD research extension, I would test all twelve assays, add other fingerprints, and check the
findings on an independent collection with compatible assays and no overlapping compounds. A
publishable study would need a clear, consistent finding about when balancing helps or harms,
supported beyond the three assays in this course project. That is the longer-term goal; the course
project will establish whether the results justify expanding the study.
